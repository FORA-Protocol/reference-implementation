Chrysippus

Chrysippus of Soli (c. 279 - 206 BCE) was the third head of the Stoa and
the thinker who gave Stoicism the shape it kept. The ancient verdict was
blunt: had there been no Chrysippus, there would have been no Stoa. He is
said to have written more than seven hundred books, and none of them
survives whole. What we know of him comes through quotation, often by
opponents, which is a measure of how much there was to oppose.

Logic

Chrysippus built the first system of propositional logic. Where Aristotle
had analysed arguments by the terms inside sentences, Chrysippus took whole
propositions as units and studied the connectives between them: if, and,
or, not. He isolated five indemonstrable argument forms, among them what is
now called modus ponens, and showed how other valid arguments reduce to
them. He treated the conditional with care, asking under what conditions
"if p then q" is true, and his answer, that the denial of q must conflict
with p, anticipates later accounts of entailment.

Physics and fate

For Chrysippus the cosmos is a single living body, pervaded by a rational
breath he called pneuma, and everything that happens in it happens by fate,
the unbroken chain of causes. The obvious objection is that fate abolishes
responsibility. His reply distinguishes kinds of cause. A cylinder pushed
on a slope rolls because it was pushed, but it rolls the way it does
because it is a cylinder. The push is the external, proximate cause; the
shape is the principal cause in the thing itself. Our assent to an
impression is likewise ours, from our own character, even though the
impression came from outside. Fate works through us, not around us.

Ethics

The good is virtue alone, and virtue is knowledge of what is and is not up
to us. Everything else, health, wealth, reputation, is indifferent, though
some indifferents are preferred and may reasonably be pursued so long as
they are not mistaken for the good. The passions are false judgements, not
brute feelings: fear is the judgement that a coming evil is unbearable, and
it is cured by correcting the judgement. The wise person is free because
nothing that can be taken away was ever counted as theirs.

Style

He was known for arguing both sides of a question so thoroughly that his
pupil Carneades, founder of the sceptical Academy, said he needed no other
source for objections to Stoicism. Chrysippus is a philosopher one meets
almost entirely through the people who tried to refute him, and who found
they had to learn his logic first.
